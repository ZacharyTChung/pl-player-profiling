\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Public football data now describes every player in a major league by dozens of per-90
measures, and a standard genre of analysis has grown around it: cluster the players, name
the clusters, and present the result as a taxonomy of roles. Published examples report
four archetypes within a single position group \citep{elfoukahi2026unveiling}, and in
basketball nine \citep{kalman2020nba} or thirteen \citep{alagappan2012from} across the
whole sport, with labels such as deep-lying progressor or advanced playmaker. The genre rests on an inference that is rarely examined. A partitioning
algorithm always returns a partition. Run $k$-means on a single multivariate Gaussian,
which by construction contains no groups at all, and it will hand back tidy clusters with
a respectable silhouette. That a method produced clusters is therefore not evidence that
clusters exist.

This paper asks how much group structure football role data actually contains, and answers
it against an explicit null. We assemble \ArcRows{} player-seasons covering
\ArcNSeasons{} seasons from \ArcSeasonFirst{} to \ArcSeasonLast{} across the
\ArcNLeagues{} major European leagues, with \ArcFeatures{} per-90 features spanning
shooting, chance creation, ball progression, touches by pitch zone, take-ons, defensive
actions and passing accuracy by distance. On that sample we run dimensionality reduction,
clustering under a selection rule fixed before any fitting, and, crucially, a calibration
against simulated data built to contain no clusters whatsoever.

Six findings are stated in advance.

First, the structure is real, and it survives the hardest null available. Against three
clusterless references, a Gaussian matched on covariance, a copula that keeps every real
marginal and correlation, and the gap statistic's uniform box, each simulated
\StrSimulations{} times, the observed silhouette at two clusters exceeds every simulation
under every null in every scope. Against the hardest of the three the margin runs from
\StrMinZFW{} standard deviations for forwards to \StrMinZAll{} for the outfield pool.
Hartigan's dip statistic on the leading component clears the hardest null by
\StrDipMinZAll{} on the pool. Football role data is not a featureless cloud. A
bootstrap, by contrast, cannot show this: clusterless data return a resampling adjusted
Rand index of at least \StrBootNullMinSingle{}, against an observed \StrBootObsMin{}, so
the stability check the taxonomy genre leans on is close to uninformative about whether
groups exist.

Second, the structure is two modes and nothing finer. The selection rule returns two
clusters globally and within every position group, on the full \ArcFeatures{} feature set
and on \ArcEligible{} eligible players. Beyond two clusters the observed silhouette
converges on the clusterless null, so the additional clusters that a taxonomy would report
are indistinguishable from what structureless data produces. Density-based clustering,
which is permitted to conclude that nothing is clustered, assigns \StrNoiseAll{} of the
outfield pool to noise and every position group entirely to noise.

Third, the two modes divide players by attacking against defensive involvement, and they
do not recover the positions the data already records. Agreement with listed position group
is \ArcARIvsPosition{} on the adjusted Rand index, far above chance and far below recovery,
and the shape of the disagreement is the point: \ArcSplitDF{} percent of defenders fall on
one side and \ArcSplitFW{} percent of forwards on the other, while midfielders divide almost
evenly between them. A supervised model reaches \SupGBMAcc{} percent accuracy on the same
features and yet routes all but \SupPoleShare{} percent of its errors through midfield, which
is the same geometry recovered by machinery that shares nothing with the clustering. This is
not a claim about one league or one season: refitted independently, every one of
\ArcNLeagues{} competitions and \ArcNSeasons{} seasons returns the same partition, and
centroids fitted in one league assign another league's players at a mean adjusted Rand index
of \RepTransferMean{}.

Fourth, two modes is not one dimension. The leading principal component carries
\ArcPCOne{} percent of the variance, which is far more than any other single direction, but
\ArcPCNinety{} components are needed to reach ninety percent of it. The space has one
dominant axis along which the density is bimodal, and a long tail of smaller axes carrying
variation that is real, interpretable and continuous. A two-mode description is a statement
about where players are concentrated, not a claim that role reduces to a single number.

Fifth, the result is robust to the feature set. A separate live sample, collected after
the largest free source of football statistics withdrew its advanced metrics in January
2026, retains only about half the features. The same analysis on that sample returns the
same answer, which is reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:reduced} as a robustness check, not as a limitation.

Sixth, the modes predict what happened. Over \OutN{} team-seasons with real results, a
club's minutes-weighted composition on the two-mode axis predicts points per match beyond
team possession, out of sample under season, league and club holdouts, raising
the coefficient of determination from \OutRSqPossSeason{} to \OutRSqBothSeason{}. The six
archetypes add nothing beyond the two modes. Real results say what the null calibration
said.

The practical implication is not that role analysis is impossible. It is that the correct
output of role analysis on this kind of data is a position along an axis rather than
membership of a type, and that a published taxonomy of a dozen archetypes is reporting
structure its data cannot support.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} places the work in the cluster
validity literature it draws on and the football literature it responds to.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the two samples and what each can support.
Section~\ref{sec:methods} gives every procedure, and states the calibration formally.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results on the primary sample first and the reduced
sample second. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} says what the two modes mean, why finer
taxonomies keep being published, and what the results do not show, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes.
