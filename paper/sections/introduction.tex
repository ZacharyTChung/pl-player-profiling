\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Public football data now describes every player in a major league by dozens of per-match
measures, and a standard genre of analysis has grown around it. Take the measures, group the
players by how similar their measures are, name the groups, and present the result as a
taxonomy of roles. Published examples report four archetypes within a single position group,
and in basketball nine or thirteen across the whole sport, with labels such as deep-lying
progressor or advanced playmaker \citep{elfoukahi2026unveiling, kalman2020nba,
alagappan2012from}. The appeal is obvious. A taxonomy turns a table of numbers into a
vocabulary a coach or a recruiter can use.

The grouping step is done by a clustering algorithm, and it is worth being explicit about what
one does, because the whole argument of this paper turns on it. A clustering algorithm is
handed a set of points, here one point per player with one coordinate per statistic, and a
number of groups to find. It then divides the points into that many groups so as to make each
group as internally similar as possible. Nothing in that procedure asks whether the points
were grouped to begin with. Handed a single cloud of points with no groups in it, a clustering
algorithm still divides the cloud, and the division it returns will still look tidy by the
measures normally used to judge one. Running the standard method on a single multivariate
Gaussian, which by construction contains no groups at all, returns two clean clusters with a
respectable separation score. Getting clusters out of a clustering algorithm is
therefore not evidence that clusters were there to begin with.

The football literature has not tested that inference, mostly because it has been busy with a
harder data problem. The most detailed work on roles uses proprietary feeds: formations and
positional roles recovered from tracking coordinates, player vectors and performance ratings
learned from event streams, and playing styles distinguished from match events
\citep{bialkowski2014large, decroos2019player, pappalardo2019playerank,
aalbers2019distinguishing}. Where public season aggregates are used instead, the standard
output is a taxonomy, whether from $k$-means on a dozen indicators or from partial membership
models on a season of counts \citep{elfoukahi2026unveiling, seri2024partial}. Two systematic
reviews catalogue the genre for players and for teams, the first synthesising twenty-six
styles from twelve studies and finding no shared framework behind them
\citep{plakias2023identifying, gu2025playing}. A review of 278 sports science articles that
used clustering found that 55 percent did not state the criterion by which the number of
clusters was chosen, which puts the omission in context \citep{fernandez2024reporting}. The
question of existence has not been answered badly so much as not asked.

Outside sport it has been asked for forty years, and the tools already exist. Choosing the
number of clusters has been studied systematically since thirty stopping rules were compared
on data with known structure, and the silhouette and the gap statistic descend from that
programme \citep{milligan1985examination, rousseeuw1987silhouettes, tibshirani2001estimating}.
The gap statistic is the direct ancestor of what this paper does, since it compares the
observed clustering against the same quantity computed on reference data drawn uniformly over
the principal box. A second tradition validates a partition by its stability under resampling,
and the theory reached a conclusion the applied literature has largely not absorbed: for
algorithms such as $k$-means, stability is governed by the geometry of the objective rather
than by the presence of clusters, so a partition can be perfectly stable on data with no
groups in it \citep{benhur2002stability, vonluxburg2010stability}. A third asks whether data
are clusterable at all, finds the formal notions of it mutually inconsistent, and recommends
tests built on Hartigan's dip statistic applied to a low dimensional projection
\citep{ackerman2009clusterability, adolfsson2019cluster, hartigan1985dip}. Underlying all
three is the position that there is no true clustering independent of the purpose a clustering
is meant to serve \citep{hennig2015true}.

The closest methodological relative of this paper is the aggregated validity index of Akhanli
and Hennig, which calibrates several criteria against their distribution over random
partitions of the same data and, applied to a season of player statistics from eight European
leagues, returned a coarse partition into five ordered groups \citep{akhanli2020comparing,
akhanli2023clustering}. That coarse partition runs along the same territorial axis reported
here. The difference is what the calibration is against. Comparing against random partitions
of the observed points asks how good a clustering is relative to other clusterings of the same
data, which is the right question for choosing among methods. Comparing against clusterless
data matched to the observed sample asks whether any clustering of these points is better than
a clustering of points with no structure in them, which is the question of existence. The two
are complementary, and the first does not answer the second. A different tradition sidesteps
the question by describing athletes as mixtures rather than as members, either as convex
combinations of a few extreme profiles or as partial members of several clusters at once
\citep{eugster2012performance, seri2024partial}. Both are natural descriptions of a continuum
and both presuppose one, since neither asks whether the data would have supported discrete
groups instead.

This paper supplies the missing test. We assemble \ArcRows{} player-seasons covering
\ArcNSeasons{} seasons across the \ArcNLeagues{} major European leagues, with \ArcFeatures{}
per-90 features spanning shooting, chance creation, ball progression, territory, take-ons and
defensive work. On that sample we run the standard pipeline, a selection rule fixed before any
fitting followed by $k$-means, and then calibrate what it returns against simulated data built
to contain no clusters at all. Three such references are used rather than one, ranging from a
Gaussian matched on covariance to a copula that keeps every real marginal distribution and
correlation, so that a reader can see which reference the claim survives. The same simulations
are used to measure what a resampling stability check is worth, which turns an assertion about
the genre's second standard piece of evidence into a number.

Our expectation, and what we find, is that the data support a real division but a far coarser
one than the field reports. A single split survives calibration in every scope, separating
players by defensive against attacking involvement, while every finer division is
indistinguishable from what structureless data produces. That split is not the listed
positions renamed, since defenders and forwards fall at opposite poles while midfielders
divide almost evenly between them; it reproduces when each season and each league is fitted
independently; and a club's composition along it predicts league points beyond possession out
of sample, while the finer archetypes predict nothing further. The practical implication is
not that role analysis is impossible. It is that the correct output of role analysis on this
kind of data is a position along an axis rather than membership of a type, and that a
published taxonomy of a dozen archetypes is reporting structure its data cannot support.
