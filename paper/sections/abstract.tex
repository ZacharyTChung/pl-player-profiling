Football analysts sort players into role archetypes such as the ball-playing centre back or
the deep-lying playmaker. Whether such types exist has never been tested, because a clustering
algorithm returns groups whatever it is given, so the usual evidence for a taxonomy is satisfied
just as well by a smooth continuum. We test existence directly on \ArcRows{} player-seasons from
\ArcNSeasons{} seasons of the big five European leagues, described by \ArcFeatures{} statistics,
by calibrating cluster quality against simulated data matched to the sample in size, dimension
and correlation but built to contain no clusters. One division survives, and it is coarse:
players separate into a defensive and an attacking mode of involvement, exceeding every
simulation under all three clusterless references in every scope, by at least
\StrMinZOverall{} standard deviations against the hardest. Nothing finer survives, and the usual
stability evidence cannot make the distinction, since clusterless data resample at an adjusted
Rand index of at least \StrBootNullMinSingle{}. The two modes are not the listed positions
renamed, agreeing with them at \ArcARIvsPosition{}, and they reproduce when each season and each
league is fitted independently. A club's composition along the axis predicts league points beyond
possession out of sample, while the finer archetypes predict nothing further. Football role space
has two poles and a populated middle, and finer taxonomies are artefacts of the method used to
find them.
