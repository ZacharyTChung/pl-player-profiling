\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What two modes mean, and what they do not}

The result is easy to overstate in either direction. It does not say that football has two
kinds of player, and it does not say that role is a single number. It says that the
distribution of players over the \ArcFeatures{} measured statistics has two regions of
concentrated density joined by a populated middle, that the axis running between them is the
dominant direction in the space, and that no division finer than that one is distinguishable
from what a partitioning algorithm produces on data containing no divisions.

The axis itself is territorial rather than technical. Its strongest loadings are touches by
pitch third and progressive receptions, not passing accuracy or duel success, and the same
ordering appears in the supervised attributions, where \ShapArcTopOne{} carries more weight
than any technical statistic for all three position groups. Football's own vocabulary for
role mixes territory with technique freely, describing a player as a ball playing centre back
or a pressing forward, but the season aggregate record is far better at recording where a
player was than what he did there. Any role structure recovered from it will be mostly a
statement about territory, and reading it as a statement about ability is the most common way
to misuse this kind of analysis.

Within position groups the two-way splits are ordered rather than categorical, which the
construction makes unavoidable: standardising within group before a two-way split forces the
centroids to be mirror images, so each split reports a single direction of variation and its
two ends. The three are not equally supported and the paper does not claim they are. The
defender split is strongest on every measure applied to it. The midfield split is well
supported with a more populated boundary. The forward split is the weakest and should be
read with the most caution: its dip test does not reject unimodality at \StrDipPFW{}, so
although its silhouette separates from the clusterless null, what it separates is plausibly a
cut through a continuum of forward involvement rather than two modes.

\subsection{Why finer taxonomies keep being published}

The methodological point generalises past football. A published role taxonomy is typically
supported by three things: a selection criterion that picks the reported number of clusters,
a stability check that shows the partition survives resampling, and centroid profiles that
read sensibly against domain knowledge. Every one of those is satisfied here by partitions
this paper shows to be artefacts.

The reason is that none of the three tests existence. A selection criterion compares
candidate partitions against each other and is never offered the option of returning none. A
bootstrap measures whether an algorithm applied twice to similar data behaves similarly,
which a deterministic procedure on a smooth unimodal distribution does very reliably, so a
high bootstrap adjusted Rand index is evidence of algorithmic stability and not of group
structure. Interpretability is the weakest of the three, because a cut through any continuous
distribution produces two centroids that differ along the direction of the cut, and a domain
expert can narrate that difference whether or not it corresponds to a real boundary. The
diagnostic evidence here makes the concern concrete rather than hypothetical: on data drawn
from a single Gaussian matched to the real covariance, the same pipeline returns a silhouette
maximum, a stable bootstrap and two nameable centroids.

Calibration against matched clusterless data fixes this at low cost. It requires no
assumption the analysis does not already make, since the simulated data are drawn using the
observed mean and covariance, it costs a few dozen refits, and it is the single step that
separates the two-mode result reported here from the six-way taxonomy the same pipeline would
have produced with the same apparent confidence. Hartigan's dip test and a density based
method permitted to answer that nothing is clustered are useful supplements, and the
agreement of all three is what makes the negative half of the result credible.

\subsection{Listed positions and data-driven roles}

The clusters do not recover listed positions, and where they disagree is the interesting
part. The division places nearly every defender on one side and nearly every forward on the
other while cutting the midfield close to in half, so it leaves the two extreme groups intact
and divides the group that sits between them. The players it reassigns are recognisable:
holding midfielders sit with the defenders and attacking midfielders sit with the forwards.

That the supervised model makes its errors in the same place is corroboration from machinery
that shares nothing with the clustering. A classifier that reaches \SupGBMAcc{} percent
accuracy while sending all but \SupPoleShare{} percent of its errors through midfield is
describing an ordered space, since a genuinely three-category problem would scatter errors
more evenly across the off-diagonal. The practical reading is that listed position is a
reasonable but coarse summary at the extremes and close to arbitrary in the middle, which is
what anyone who has watched a modern midfield would expect and which the data now supports
quantitatively.

\subsection{Possession adjustment and its asymmetry}

The possession adjustment result is independent of the clustering and probably the most
directly reusable finding here. Defensive counts are conventionally adjusted as though
opportunity scales inversely with possession, so that a team with twice the ball concedes
half the defensive events. The elasticities measured from the data run from
\ArcElastTackles{} for tackles to \ArcElastClearances{} for clearances, all below one and
most far below it, so the conventional correction overcorrects every statistic it touches and
does so unequally across statistics. A correction applied at unit elasticity converts a
defensive-count comparison between a possession-dominant club and a counter-attacking one
from biased in one direction to biased in the other.

The adjustment is also genuinely one sided, which is worth stating because the symmetry
argument is superficially compelling. If defensive opportunity falls with possession then
attacking opportunity should rise with it, and correcting only one side looks arbitrary. The
counterfactual reported in Section~\ref{sec:results:symmetric} tests this and rejects it:
applying the correction in both directions makes the description worse. Attacking output does
not scale with team possession the way the symmetric account requires, because a player's
attacking opportunity depends on where his team uses the ball rather than only on how much of
it it has, and a single elasticity cannot represent that.

\subsection{Goalkeepers}

Goalkeepers are analysed separately because they share almost no features with outfielders,
and the archive restores the full block that makes the analysis possible at all. The division
that emerges runs along what a goalkeeper is asked to do rather than how well he does it. The
situational axis carries an effect size of \ArcKeeperEtaFullSituation{} against
\ArcKeeperEtaFullTechnique{} for technique, so a partition of goalkeepers built from these
statistics is mostly a partition of the teams in front of them and of the sweeping duties
those teams impose. This is a caution for anyone building goalkeeper archetypes from public
data, since the resulting labels will describe a role assignment made by a coach rather than a
property of the goalkeeper, and it is easy to present the former as the latter.

\subsection{Limitations}

Season aggregates discard context. A tackle count does not say where the tackles happened, an
expected goals total does not distinguish six poor chances from two good ones, and a touch
map summarised into three thirds is a coarse instrument for a question about territory that
turns out to be territorial. Everything here is therefore a claim about what season aggregate
data can support, not about what football contains, and the two should not be conflated: it
remains entirely possible that finer roles exist and that this description cannot see them.
Event and tracking data would be the way to find out.

The confound that possession adjustment does not remove is team strength. Correcting the
defensive counts removes the exposure effect from those five features, but the attacking
statistics still scale with a team's own share of the ball, and the leading component remains
related to team quality. Correcting both directions would require an instrument this data
cannot supply, so the residual is acknowledged rather than solved. Per ninety rates remain
noisy above the eligibility threshold, which is why the outlier list of
Section~\ref{sec:results:outliers} carries a minutes caveat and why rankings computed on
partial seasons should be shrunk before they are read.

The archive is a mirror rather than the source, and although its coverage was verified
against the surviving public tables where those overlap, it cannot be re-derived from the
live site. One season was excluded outright because a whole feature block was absent from it.
Season aggregation also assumes a player's role is constant across a season, which the drift
analysis shows holds for most players and fails for a minority. Finally, the two-mode result
covers recent European football at the top level, and the five competitions tested resemble
one another more than they resemble the rest of the sport.

\subsection{Applications}

For recruitment the honest use of this work is narrow and real. The involvement axis is
stable enough to support questions about squad composition, such as whether a club's minutes
are concentrated at one end of the distribution, and the club level signatures make that
legible. What the work does not support is a scouting taxonomy: a shortlist built by matching
a target's archetype label will be matching a label that the data does not establish, and the
similarity search over the continuous profile is the better instrument for the same question
because it does not force a boundary that is not there.

For anyone doing quantitative football analysis the two transferable items are the possession
elasticities, which can be applied directly to any defensive count comparison, and the null
calibration, which costs little and should be run before any archetype claim is made.

\subsection{Future work}

The obvious extension is data. Event and tracking data would restore the spatial detail that
season aggregates discard and would test the two-mode claim against a description rich enough
to reveal finer structure if it exists, at the cost of the reproducibility a public source
provides. The most interesting version of that test is the one this paper cannot run: whether
the modes are a property of football or a property of the measurement, which requires a
dataset where a finer taxonomy is known to be present.

Within public data, three directions follow. The role drift analysis covers one club and
would be worth extending, which is a matter of scraping time rather than method. Extending
beyond the big five, to competitions with different possession profiles, would test whether
the elasticities and the two-mode result hold where the league is less concentrated. Modelling
attacking exposure properly, rather than through a single elasticity, would let the symmetric
adjustment be revisited on a footing the current test cannot provide.
