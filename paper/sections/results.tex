\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Is there structure at all?}
\label{sec:results:structure}

The first question is not how many clusters there are but whether there are any, and it is
answered against the null described in Section~\ref{sec:methods:null}: \StrSimulations{}
synthetic datasets matched to the observed feature matrix in size, dimension and covariance
but drawn from a single Gaussian and therefore containing no groups whatsoever.

The answer is that real structure exists, and that it is confined to a single division.
Figure~\ref{fig:structurenull} shows the comparison. At two clusters the observed
silhouette is \StrObsAll{} for the outfield pool against a null mean of \StrNullAll{}, a
standard score of \StrZAll{}, and the observed value exceeds every one of the
\StrSimulations{} simulations. The same holds within each position group: \StrObsDF{}
against \StrNullDF{} for defenders, \StrObsMF{} against \StrNullMF{} for midfielders and
\StrObsFW{} against \StrNullFW{} for forwards, with standard scores from \StrZFW{} to
\StrZDF{} and no simulation reaching the observed value in any scope.

Beyond two clusters the two curves converge. By around six clusters the observed silhouette
is inside the simulated band in every position group, which is the central negative result
of this paper stated precisely: the third, fourth and subsequent clusters that a taxonomy
would name are not distinguishable from what a partitioning algorithm extracts from
structureless data.

Three further instruments agree. Hartigan's dip test rejects unimodality on the leading
principal component at $p\,\StrDipPAll{}$ for the outfield pool, $p\,\StrDipPDF{}$ for
defenders and $p\,\StrDipPMF{}$ for midfielders, so the dominant axis is genuinely
two-moded rather than merely wide. Forwards are the exception, returning
$p=\StrDipPFW{}$, which says the forward split is real by the silhouette calibration but
that its leading axis is not detectably bimodal, and both halves of that are reported.
Density-based clustering assigns \StrNoiseAll{} of the outfield pool to noise and every
position group entirely to noise, which is what a density method does when handed a
continuum with a density gradient rather than separated groups. The Gaussian mixture
criterion declines monotonically to the top of the candidate range in every scope, which is
the signature of a model absorbing extra components into a smooth density rather than
identifying real ones, and it is reported for completeness rather than treated as
selecting a number.

Taken together the instruments support one claim and refuse a stronger one. Football role
data contains a real division into two modes. It does not contain a taxonomy.

\subsection{How many dimensions the space has}
\label{sec:results:dimension}

A two-mode description invites a natural objection: if the data reduce to two groups,
perhaps they reduce to one number, and the whole exercise is an elaborate way of
rediscovering an attacking-to-defending scale that any observer could have drawn by hand.
Principal components on the full \ArcFeatures{} archive features answer that directly, and
the answer is no.

The leading component carries \ArcPCOne{} percent of the variance, which is far more than any other
single direction and confirms that one axis dominates. It is nowhere near the whole space.
The first three components together reach only \ArcPCThree{} percent, \ArcPCKaiser{} components
exceed the Kaiser criterion, and \ArcPCNinety{} are required to reach ninety percent of the
variance. The role space therefore has one dominant axis and a long tail of smaller ones,
which is a different object from a line.

The content of the leading components is readable and matches the football. The first
separates players by where on the pitch they touch the ball, loading positively on
progressive receptions and touches in the attacking third and the opposition box, and
negatively on touches in the defensive third and the defending team's own box. The second
is a progression axis that is close to orthogonal to the first, loading on progressive
passes, progressive carries and passes into the final third while loading negatively on
non-penalty expected goals and touches in the opposition box. A deep midfielder who moves
the ball forward and a striker who receives it in the box sit at opposite ends of the
second component while sharing similar territory on neither.

That pair explains why two modes coexist with a genuinely multidimensional space. The first
component is bimodal and drives the partition. The components behind it carry real and
interpretable variation that is continuous rather than clustered, which is precisely the
variation a finer taxonomy would have to divide and, as
Section~\ref{sec:results:structure} showed, cannot.

\subsection{What the two modes are}
\label{sec:results:archetypes}

Two modes within each of three position groups gives six descriptions, and with
\ArcFeatures{} features they can be characterised far more sharply than a reduced feature
set allows. Names were assigned only after inspecting centroid profiles and member players,
and the justifications quoted here are generated from the computed centroids rather than
composed for the paper. Figure~\ref{fig:arcprofiles} shows the deviation profiles and
Figure~\ref{fig:arcradar} the radar comparisons.

Among defenders the division is between \ArcNDFZero{} \ArcNameDFZero{} and \ArcNDFOne{}
\ArcNameDFOne{}. The first take three times as many touches in their own defensive third as
the second, more than twice as many in their own box, twice the possession-adjusted
clearances and win considerably more aerial duels. The second take five times as many
touches in the attacking third, attempt ten times as many crosses, receive far more
progressive passes and attempt four times as many take-ons. This is the centre back against
wide defender distinction, and the notable point is that the source's own position label
does not encode it: the great majority of both groups are listed simply as defenders.

Among midfielders the division separates \ArcNMFZero{} \ArcNameMFZero{} from \ArcNMFOne{}
\ArcNameMFOne{}. The first recover the ball more often, tackle more, and take far more
touches in their own third. The instructive detail is that they also make more passes into
the final third and more progressive passes than the second group. They are not less
involved in progressing the ball, they progress it differently: by passing forward from
deep rather than by receiving high. A description that treated attacking involvement as one
axis would miss that, and it is the clearest case in the paper of the richer feature set
buying something the reduced one could not.

Among forwards the division separates \ArcNFWZero{} \ArcNameFWZero{} from \ArcNFWOne{}
\ArcNameFWOne{}, and it is not a division by scoring. Non-penalty expected goals are
slightly higher in the first group. What separates them is carrying and creation: the second
group record nearly three times the progressive carries, almost three times the passes into
the box, twice the take-ons and substantially more shot creating actions, while shooting
slightly less. Forward is not one role, but the axis that splits it is involvement in
build-up rather than goal threat.

One structural property constrains how far these six can be read as independent. Features
are standardised within position group before a two-way split, so the two centroids in a
group are exactly anti-parallel and the larger cluster necessarily carries the smaller
deviations. Each pair is therefore the two ends of one axis, and the six archetypes describe
three axes rather than six independently shaped profiles.

\subsection{The modes are not the positions}
\label{sec:results:notposition}

The obvious deflationary reading of a two-mode result is that the algorithm has rediscovered
the difference between defending and attacking, which nobody needed clustering to learn. The
contingency between the pooled partition and listed position group rules that reading out,
though not in the direction the deflationary account expects.

Agreement is well above chance and well below recovery. The adjusted Rand index against
listed position group is \ArcARIvsPosition{} and the normalised mutual information is
\ArcNMIvsPosition{}, with cluster purity at \ArcPurityVsPosition{} percent against a
three-class problem. Scoring against the finer listed position instead of the group is lower
still, at \ArcARIvsPositionFull{}. A partition that simply relabelled position would return
values near one, and a partition unrelated to position would return values near zero. These
sit in between, which means the two modes divide the same players along a different line.

Where that line falls is the informative part. Of the players in the defensive mode,
\ArcModeDefFW{} are listed as forwards, out of \ArcModeDefDF{} defenders and
\ArcModeDefMF{} midfielders; the attacking mode contains \ArcModeAttFW{} forwards against
\ArcModeAttDF{} defenders. The poles are close to pure. \ArcSplitDF{} percent of defenders
fall on one side and \ArcSplitFW{} percent of forwards on the other. Midfielders divide \ArcModeAttMF{} to \ArcModeDefMF{}, which puts \ArcSplitMF{}
percent of them on the attacking side and is as close to an even split as the sample permits, and it is that even split that drags the agreement indices
down.

The partition is therefore not a relabelling of position but a cut through it. Defenders and
forwards sit at the ends of the same axis and are assigned almost deterministically;
midfielders are spread along its middle and are assigned by which end they lean toward.
Section~\ref{sec:results:supervised} shows the same geometry from the supervised side, where
the classifier confuses midfielders with both poles and almost never confuses the poles with
each other. Two methods with no shared machinery agree that the space is an ordered
continuum whose density is concentrated at two ends, and that listed position is a coarse
and partly arbitrary partition of it.

\subsection{Do the modes describe stable roles?}
\label{sec:results:archestability}

A partition that changed every season would describe a season rather than a role. Tracking
\ArcTracked{} players across the sample, of whom \ArcTrackedTwo{} appear in two or more
seasons, membership changes in \ArcSwitchRate{} percent of consecutive season pairs against
a within-group chance baseline near half. The adjusted Rand index between consecutive
seasons runs from \ArcSeasonARIMin{} to \ArcSeasonARIMax{} with a mean of
\ArcSeasonARIMean{}.

Refitting the partition independently within each league and comparing against the pooled
one gives a mean adjusted Rand index of \ArcLeagueARI{}, and refitting within each season
gives \ArcSeasonRefitARI{}. Bootstrap resampling returns \ArcBootDF{} for defenders,
\ArcBootMF{} for midfielders and \ArcBootFW{} for forwards. The two modes are a property of
how players play rather than of which season or competition they were measured in.

The three groups are not equally well supported and the paper does not claim they are. The
defender split is the strongest: the largest centroid separations, the lowest churn, the
highest agreement across leagues, and a dip test that rejects unimodality on the defender
axis. The midfield split is well supported with a more populated boundary. The forward split
is the weakest, and should be read with more caution than the other two: its dip test does
not reject unimodality, the division is uneven, the low involvement group has no positive
signature of its own, and one league's refit falls well below the others. Silhouette does
separate it from the clusterless null, so there is real structure, but it reads more like a
cut through a continuum of forward involvement than like two modes.

\subsection{The same solution in every season and every league}
\label{sec:results:replication}

A partition that appears once is a description of one dataset. A partition that appears
independently in five seasons and five competitions is a description of the sport. The
archive supports the stronger test, so each season and each league is standardised inside
itself and clustered from scratch, borrowing no centre, no scale and no starting point from
the pooled fit.

Every scope recovers the pooled partition. The weakest season agrees with the pool at an
adjusted Rand index of \RepSeasonPooledMin{} and the weakest league at
\RepLeaguePooledMin{}, on \RepN{} player seasons in total. The separating axis is the same
object each time rather than merely a similarly sized one: the correlation between each
scope's own discriminant direction and the pooled direction never falls below
\RepAxisCorrMin{}. Bundesliga, the league with the lowest agreement, is also the smallest
sample here, and its axis correlation is indistinguishable from the rest.

Importing one scope's centroids into another scope's players separates replication of the
solution from change in the players, and it is the cleaner test of the two. Across the
twenty ordered league pairs the transferred centroids reproduce the receiving league's own
fit at a mean adjusted Rand index of \RepTransferMean{}, never falling below
\RepTransferMin{}. A model of role fitted on Ligue 1 assigns Bundesliga players almost
exactly as a model fitted on Bundesliga does. The two modes are not a quirk of one league's
tactical fashion.

The season-against-season comparison on players present in both seasons is lower, at a mean
of \RepPairMean{} with a range from \RepPairMin{} to \RepPairMax{}, and the pattern in
Figure~\ref{fig:archive_replication} explains why. Agreement decays monotonically with the
gap between seasons, so adjacent seasons agree most and the seasons furthest apart agree
least, while the transfer index over the same pairs stays flat. That decay is players changing role over four years rather than a weakness of the
partition, and the player-level view confirms it: across the same pairs \RepShareMean{}
percent of players present in both seasons are assigned to the same mode in both, falling
no lower than \RepShareMin{} percent on the pair furthest apart in time. Roughly one
player in twelve moves between modes over a four-year span, which is a plausible rate of
genuine role change and not the churn an unstable partition would produce.

\subsection{Position seen through the two modes}
\label{sec:results:supervised}

The unsupervised result says the space has two poles. A supervised model gives an
independent way to check that claim, because if the space really is a continuum with two
ends then the two ends should be easy to tell apart and the middle should be hard, and the
errors should fall in a specific place rather than spread evenly.

Listed position group is highly predictable from the \ArcFeatures{} features. Gradient
boosting reaches \SupGBMAcc{} percent accuracy and a macro F1 of \SupGBMF{} out of fold against a
majority-class baseline of \SupBaseline{} percent, and multinomial logistic regression is barely
behind at \SupLogitAcc{} percent and \SupLogitF{}. The near equality of a linear model and a boosted
tree ensemble is itself informative, because it means almost all of the recoverable signal
is linearly separable and there is little interaction structure left for the trees to find.
A taxonomy of many discrete types would be expected to leave more.

The errors are where the argument is. Defenders and forwards are almost never mistaken for
one another: of \SupN{} player seasons the model assigns \SupDFasFW{} defenders to the
forward class and \SupFWasDF{} forwards to the defender class. Every other confusion runs
through midfield, which is involved in \SupMiddleErrors{} errors against the
\SupPoleErrors{} that link the two poles directly, so only \SupPoleShare{} percent of all
misclassifications skip the middle. Midfielders are recovered at \SupMFRecall{} percent and forwards at
\SupFWRecall{} percent, against \SupDFRecall{} percent for defenders, with per-class F1 of \SupMFF{},
\SupFWF{} and \SupDFF{} respectively. This is the confusion structure of an ordered line
whose ends are far apart and whose middle overlaps both, and it is not the confusion
structure of three distinct categories, which would scatter errors more evenly across the
off-diagonal.

The feature attributions agree. The three strongest features overall are
\ShapArcTopOne{}, \ShapArcTopTwo{} and \ShapArcTopThree{}, all of which are territorial
rather than technical, and touches in the defensive third alone carries more attribution
than any other feature for all three classes. Position, as the data record it, is mostly a
statement about where a player stands. The per-class panels in
Figure~\ref{fig:archive_shap_by_class} add a detail the aggregate hides: defenders are
identified overwhelmingly by what they do, with four of their five strongest features
positively signed, while forwards and midfielders are identified as much by the absence of
defensive volume as by the presence of attacking output. A class defined largely by
negations is a class occupying one end of a scale.

\subsection{Players the description fits worst}
\label{sec:results:outliers}

A two-mode description of a continuum will fit some players badly, and which players those
are is a test of whether the description is measuring football or noise. Isolation forests
and Mahalanobis distance under a shrinkage covariance were fitted separately within each
position group, and the two detectors agree on \ArcOutAgreement{} percent of the players
they flag.

The \ArcOutN{} strongest outliers are \ArcOutDistinct{} distinct players, headed by
\ArcOutTop{}. Two features of the list are worth reporting. The first is that
\ArcOutCompound{} of the \ArcOutN{} carry a compound listed position such as midfielder and
forward together, against a much smaller share in the pool, so the players the partition
handles worst are the ones the listing system also declines to place. The second is that the
detector is not simply finding stars: it also finds attacking full backs whose output
profile resembles a winger's, which is the same boundary the defender split describes at
Section~\ref{sec:results:archetypes} and a genuine tactical category rather than an artefact.

One caveat belongs with the list rather than after it. \ArcOutShortMinutes{} of the
\ArcOutN{} played under nine hundred minutes in the season concerned, so their per ninety
rates carry more sampling noise than a full season's would, and extreme rates are easier to
reach on a small denominator. The eligibility threshold removes the worst of this and does
not remove all of it, and an outlier list is exactly the place where the residual matters
most. The list should therefore be read as evidence about the kind of player the two-mode
description struggles with, and not as a ranking in which the fifteenth entry means what the
first does.

\subsection{Correcting defensive counts for possession}
\label{sec:results:padj}

Before any structure is estimated, the defensive counts have to be separated from the
opportunity to record them. Team defensive volume does rise as opponent possession rises, so
the confound is real, and the conventional correction assumes the relationship is
proportional: a side with twice the ball is taken to concede half the defensive events.

It is much weaker than that. Fitted on the primary sample, over \ArcPadjTeamSeasons{}
team-seasons, all \ArcElastCount{} elasticities fall below one and most fall far below it:
\ArcElastTackles{} for tackles, \ArcElastInterceptions{} for interceptions,
\ArcElastBlocks{} for blocks, \ArcElastFouls{} for fouls committed and
\ArcElastClearances{} for clearances. Clearances are the one statistic that approaches
proportionality, which is what one would expect of the most purely reactive action in the
set, and tackles are furthest from it, which is what one would expect of the most
volitional. The spread from \ArcElastMin{} to \ArcElastMax{} is itself the point: a single
exponent applied to every defensive statistic is wrong even in the cases where some
correction is warranted.

The reduced sample supplies the check the primary sample cannot, because it retains the
team-level possession series needed to measure what is left after correcting. Across its
\PadjTeamSeasons{} team-seasons, team possession ranges from \PadjPossessionMin{} to
\PadjPossessionMax{} percent, and its fitted elasticities agree with the primary sample in
falling well below one, at \ElasticityInterceptions{} for interceptions,
\ElasticityTackles{} for tackles won and \ElasticityFouls{} for fouls committed.
Table~\ref{tab:padj} shows what each choice of exponent leaves behind. The three raw correlations
with team possession are \PadjCorrRawInterceptions{}, \PadjCorrRawTackles{} and
\PadjCorrRawFouls{}, all negative, since a player whose team keeps the ball records fewer
defensive actions. Applying the conventional exponent overshoots every one of them: the
correlations become \PadjCorrUnitInterceptions{}, \PadjCorrUnitTackles{} and
\PadjCorrUnitFouls{}, reversing sign in all three cases and, for fouls committed, ending
further from zero than where it started. The correction passes through the confound and out
the other side. Adjusting with the measured elasticity instead leaves
\PadjCorrAdjInterceptions{}, \PadjCorrAdjTackles{} and \PadjCorrAdjFouls{}, which are
three to four times closer to zero than the conventional exponent achieves and are the
smallest residual dependence available from either choice.

Two qualifications belong with this. The residual-dependence check runs on one league,
whose possession shares are relatively compressed, so the exact residuals should not be
transported to a competition with a wider spread without refitting. The elasticities
themselves are fitted across \ArcNLeagues{} leagues and \ArcNSeasons{} seasons and are less
exposed to that objection. More importantly, adjusting the
defensive counts does not make the feature set as a whole team-neutral, because the
attacking statistics still scale with a team's own share of the ball. What follows should
be read as having removed one identifiable confound, not all of them.

\subsection{Why the correction is one sided}
\label{sec:results:symmetric}

Correcting only the defensive statistics invites an obvious objection. Attacking output
also depends on possession, so the feature set is still team dependent and the correction
looks like half a correction. The objection is worth testing rather than answering by
assertion, and the test produces the sharpest methodological result in the paper.

This test runs on the reduced sample, which is the only one of the two carrying an
independent expected-goals source with involvement measures, and its conclusion is about the
shape of the two relationships rather than about their exact magnitudes.

The two sides of the ball do not behave alike. Fitted the same way, against a team's own
possession rather than the opponent's, the attacking elasticities are \BetaShots{} for
shots, \BetaNPXG{} for non-penalty expected goals, \BetaGoals{} for non-penalty goals,
\BetaAssists{} for assists, \BetaXGChain{} for xGChain and \BetaXGBuildup{} for
xGBuildup. Every one of them except crossing, at \BetaCrosses{}, exceeds one, while every
defensive elasticity falls well below it. Figure~\ref{fig:elasticities} shows the two
families either side of the proportional line.

That asymmetry is not a curiosity, it is the whole argument. For defending, opponent
possession is an exposure denominator in the plain sense that a tackle cannot occur while
your own side holds the ball, and the sub-proportional response says exposure is exactly
what it measures. For attacking, an elasticity above one says the opposite: ten percent
more of the ball buys appreciably more than ten percent more output. Returns to possession
are increasing. That is a statement about football rather than about measurement, so
dividing it out would remove signal along with any bias. The two Understat involvement
measures make the point most starkly, since both exceed two: a side that keeps the ball
does not merely have more possessions, its players are involved in more of each.

The counterfactual was nonetheless computed. Applying the fitted exponents to the attacking
block as well moves the correlation between club centroids and final league position from
\SymCorrBaseline{} to \SymCorrAdjusted{}, so a symmetric correction does remove a real
part of the team relationship rather than nothing at all. It does not remove all of it, the
partition moves at an adjusted Rand index of \SymARI{} against the one reported here, and
the silhouette drifts from \SymSilhouetteBase{} to \SymSilhouetteAdj{}. The choice is
therefore a judgement rather than a fact, and it is recorded as one: we treat possession
driven productivity as something a role profile should reflect, not something to divide
away, and a reader who takes the opposite view has the numbers to see what that would cost.

\subsection{Goalkeepers}
\label{sec:results:keepers}

Goalkeepers are described by a different vocabulary from outfield players, and the primary
sample supplies all of it: \ArcKeeperSeasons{} keeper-seasons with post-shot expected
goals, sweeper actions and their average distance, cross stopping, launch rate and average
pass length, at a worst per-column coverage of \ArcKeeperCoverageMin{}. Post-shot expected
goals minus goals allowed is the proper measure of saves above expectation, because it
conditions on the difficulty of the shots faced rather than only their number.

The first attempt at a partition reproduces a trap worth reporting. Clustering on all
available features returns \ArcKeeperK{} clusters at a silhouette of \ArcKeeperSilFull{},
and the partition looks interpretable until it is asked what it is made of. Membership
explains a mean of \ArcKeeperEtaFullSituation{} of the variance in situational quantities,
the volume and quality of shooting a defence permits, against \ArcKeeperEtaFullTechnique{}
in quantities describing what the goalkeeper does. The partition is largely a ranking of the
teams in front of the goalkeepers. Named as archetypes it would be a league table wearing a
tactical vocabulary.

Excluding the exposure variables and clustering on technique alone reverses that
completely. The same rule again returns \ArcKeeperK{} clusters, at a silhouette of
\ArcKeeperSilTech{} and a bootstrap adjusted Rand index of \ArcKeeperBootARI{}, and now
membership explains \ArcKeeperEtaTechTechnique{} of the variance in technique against
\ArcKeeperEtaTechSituation{} in situation, a ratio of about seven. This partition describes
goalkeepers.

What it describes is not the expected taxonomy. The familiar trio of shot stopper, sweeper
keeper and distributor is not what the data produces. The division is between
\ArcKeeperNameZero{}s, \ArcKeeperNZero{} keeper-seasons who stay on their line, and
\ArcKeeperNameOne{}s, \ArcKeeperNOne{} who come off it, separating on cross stopping and
sweeping together. Distribution style barely separates the clusters at all, and neither
does shot stopping ability. Both are real and measurable, and both are continua lying
across the off-line division rather than dimensions of it. A goalkeeper who sweeps is not
thereby a better or worse shot stopper, and how far he kicks the ball is close to
independent of both.

That is a substantive finding rather than a taxonomy, and it echoes the outfield result: the
data supports one binary distinction plus continuous variation, not a set of types.

ranking and the partition.

\subsection*{Analyses on the reduced sample}

The two subsections that follow use the reduced sample of
Section~\ref{sec:data:reduced}, the Premier League for the seasons ending 2025 and 2026,
which retains roughly half the features. The first asks whether the main result survives that
description, since a finding that holds on both is worth more than one that holds on either
alone. The second reports a property of the feature set that does not depend on the
clustering at all. The remaining reduced-sample analyses, which support the argument without
carrying it, are in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:reducedanalyses}.

\subsection{Robustness: the same result on half the features}
\label{sec:results:reduced}

The primary sample describes each player with \ArcFeatures{} features. A natural worry is
that the two-mode result depends on that description, and in particular on the expected
goals and possession blocks that dominate it. The second sample of
Section~\ref{sec:data:reduced} provides an unusually clean test, because the withdrawal
removed a specific and large part of the feature set without touching the population: the
same competition, the same kind of players, roughly half the features.

Repeating the analysis there returns the same answer. The declared rule selects two
clusters globally and within every position group, agreement with listed positions remains
weak at \ARIvsPosition{}, and bootstrap stability of the global partition is
\BootARIGlobal{}. Density-based clustering again assigns every position group entirely to
noise. Removing expected goals, all passing and possession volume, shot and goal creating
actions and the entire advanced goalkeeping table changes what the two modes are made of
without changing that there are two of them.

That is informative in both directions. It supports the central claim, since a result that
survives halving its own feature set is not an artefact of any particular measurement. It
also bounds what richer data buys: the modern feature block sharpens the description of the
two modes considerably, as Section~\ref{sec:results:archetypes} shows, but it does not
reveal additional modes that the poorer description was hiding.

The remaining reduced-sample analyses are reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:reducedanalyses}. Several of them, in particular the
within-season role drift of Section~\ref{sec:results:drift}, depend on match-level data
that exists only for that window, which is why they are retained at all.

\subsection{Statistics that change meaning by position}
\label{sec:results:divergence}

The correlation analysis produced the clearest substantive finding available from the
reduced feature set. Correlations were computed within each position group and compared
across groups, and the ten pairs whose correlation varies most between groups are
reported in Table~\ref{tab:divergence} and shown in Figure~\ref{fig:divergence}. The
largest spread between position groups reaches \CorrSpreadMax{}, against a median spread
across all pairs of \CorrSpreadMedian{}.

Every one of the ten most divergent pairs changes sign between position groups. The
relationship between shots on target and crosses is positive for midfielders and negative
for forwards, which is to say that a midfielder who crosses often is generally a more
active attacker while a forward who crosses often is generally not the player getting on
the end of chances. The relationship between key passes and tackles won is positive for
defenders and negative for midfielders, distinguishing the full back whose defensive work
and creative output rise together from the midfield division of labour between a
destroyer and a creator.

The practical implication is that a single pooled correlation matrix, of the kind that
appears routinely in public football analysis, averages over these sign reversals and
reports a number that describes no actual population of players. It also supplies the
central justification for the position stratified analysis that follows, since a global
embedding fitted across all outfield players is fitted across relationships that point in
opposite directions.
