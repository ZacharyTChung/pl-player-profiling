\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{What survived the withdrawal}
\label{sec:results:availability}

The audit reported in Table~\ref{tab:availability} establishes the scale of the loss. In
the goalkeeping detail table, \NKeeperAdvEmpty{} of \NKeeperAdvStatCols{} statistical
columns are entirely empty in both seasons, leaving three: nineties played, goals
conceded and penalties conceded. Neither of the two surviving substantive columns
supports any of the analyses that table exists to enable. The pattern is the same across
the outfield tables, where passing, possession, and goal and shot creation retain their
headers and lose their contents.

This is the first result of the paper, and it is a methodological one. A study that
selected its features from the published schema of this source would produce an empty
feature matrix while every column name resolved correctly, and the failure would surface
only as unexplained missingness far downstream. Feature selection against observed values
rather than published schemas is now a necessary step when working with public football
data.

\subsection{Descriptive structure}
\label{sec:results:descriptive}

After filtering, \NEligiblePrimary{} outfield players are eligible in the primary season,
comprising \NDFPrimary{} defenders, \NMFPrimary{} midfielders and \NFWPrimary{} forwards.
Summary statistics by position group appear in Table~\ref{tab:summary}, and the
distributions of four representative statistics are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:distributions}.

The feature set is strongly non-normal. All \NFeaturesRejectNormal{} of the
\NFeatures{} features reject the Shapiro-Wilk null at the five percent level, mean
absolute skewness is \SkewMeanAbs{}, and the most skewed feature is goals per shot at
\SkewMax{}. Mardia's test rejects multivariate normality decisively on both the skewness
and kurtosis components. Principal component analysis requires no distributional
assumption to be computed, so this does not invalidate the decomposition, but it does
mean the components should be read as directions of variance rather than as latent
factors with any distributional interpretation, and it justifies the use of Spearman
rather than Pearson correlation throughout.

\subsection{Statistics that change meaning by position}
\label{sec:results:divergence}

The correlation analysis produced the clearest substantive finding available from the
reduced feature set. Correlations were computed within each position group and compared
across groups, and the ten pairs whose correlation varies most between groups are
reported in Table~\ref{tab:divergence} and shown in Figure~\ref{fig:divergence}. The
largest spread between position groups reaches \CorrSpreadMax{}, against a median spread
across all pairs of \CorrSpreadMedian{}.

Every one of the ten most divergent pairs changes sign between position groups. The
relationship between shots on target and crosses is positive for midfielders and negative
for forwards, which is to say that a midfielder who crosses often is generally a more
active attacker while a forward who crosses often is generally not the player getting on
the end of chances. The relationship between key passes and tackles won is positive for
defenders and negative for midfielders, distinguishing the full back whose defensive work
and creative output rise together from the midfield division of labour between a
destroyer and a creator.

The practical implication is that a single pooled correlation matrix, of the kind that
appears routinely in public football analysis, averages over these sign reversals and
reports a number that describes no actual population of players. It also supplies the
central justification for the position stratified analysis that follows, since a global
embedding fitted across all outfield players is fitted across relationships that point in
opposite directions.

\subsection{Comparing embeddings}
\label{sec:results:embeddings}

Principal component analysis, UMAP and t-SNE were fitted to the same standardised
features and compared in Figure~\ref{fig:embeddings}. The selected UMAP configuration
used \UMAPBestNeighbors{} neighbours at a minimum distance of \UMAPBestMinDist{}, chosen
by trustworthiness over the twelve configuration grid reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:umap}.

The three methods agree on the dominant structure and differ in how they present it.
Trustworthiness is \TrustPCA{} for the linear projection, \TrustUMAP{} for UMAP and
\TrustTSNE{} for t-SNE, while continuity is close to identical across the three. Nearest
neighbour position purity at ten neighbours is \PurityPCA{} for the linear projection and
\PurityUMAP{} for UMAP, so roughly two thirds to three quarters of a player's closest
statistical neighbours share his listed position group. That figure is informative in
both directions: it confirms that the surviving features carry real positional signal,
and it establishes that a substantial minority of every player's nearest neighbours are
listed at a different position, which is the space in which data-driven roles can differ
from listed ones.

All three embeddings display a continuous gradient from defenders through midfielders to
forwards rather than separated groups. That gradient, and not a set of islands, is the
honest description of the geometry, and it conditions how the clustering that follows
should be read.

\subsection{How many roles the data supports}
\label{sec:results:clustering}

Applying the consensus rule declared in Section~\ref{sec:methods} to the four selection
criteria gives $k=\KGlobal{}$ for the global clustering and the same value within each of
the three position groups. The full criterion curves appear in
Figure~\ref{fig:kselection} and Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix:kselection}. This is the second
principal result of the paper. The surviving public feature set supports a binary
division within each position group and nothing finer. Requesting more clusters produces
partitions that the criteria do not support and that resampling does not reproduce.

Stability separates the position groups sharply. Across \BootstrapN{} bootstrap resamples
the mean adjusted Rand index against the reference partition is \BootARIGlobal{} globally
and \BootARIMF{} for midfielders, both of which describe a partition that reappears almost
unchanged under resampling. For defenders the mean falls to \BootARIDF{} with a standard
deviation of \BootARISDDF{}, and for forwards to \BootARIFW{} with a standard deviation of
\BootARISDFW{}. The minimum adjusted Rand index observed across forward resamples is
\BootARIMinFW{}, which is to say that in the least favourable resample the partition
recovered is no better than chance. Forwards number only \NFWPrimary{} players above the
minutes threshold, so this is partly a sample size problem, but it is a real limit on what
can be claimed. The consensus co-assignment structure is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:consensus}.

The relationship between discovered clusters and listed positions is weak. The global
partition scores an adjusted Rand index of \ARIvsPosition{} against the four-way position
group with a cluster purity of \PurityVsPosition{}, so the clusters are not a rediscovery
of the positions that the source already records. Figure~\ref{fig:clustervsposition} shows
where the two disagree. Read together with the embedding geometry, the interpretation is
that the dominant axis in the surviving data is not defender against forward but
attacking involvement against defensive involvement, which cuts across the listed groups
and places a ball-playing full back nearer an ordinary midfielder than a defensive
counterpart at his own listed position.

\subsection{Goalkeepers}
\label{sec:results:keepers}

The goalkeeper analysis is the clearest illustration of what the withdrawal cost.
\NKeeperAdvEmpty{} of the \NKeeperAdvStatCols{} statistical columns in the advanced
goalkeeping table are empty, and the \NKeeperAdvUsable{} that survive are duplicated in
the basic table, so the advanced table contributes nothing whatsoever. Of the five
composite axes the study set out to build, \KeeperAxesBuilt{} can be constructed and
\KeeperAxesLost{} cannot. No proxy is substituted for the missing four, because no
surviving column measures sweeping, distribution or cross handling even approximately.

With post-shot expected goals unavailable, shot stopping is estimated by regressing saves
on shots on target faced and treating the residual as saves above volume. The regression
returns a coefficient of determination of \KeeperSavesRSq{}, which is high almost by
construction, since every shot on target is either saved or conceded. The informative
quantity is the residual spread, a standard error of \KeeperSavesResidSE{} saves across
the season. This index adjusts for how much work a goalkeeper receives but not for how
hard that work is, which is precisely what post-shot expected goals provided, so it should
be read as a weak substitute rather than an equivalent.

Save percentage shows no relationship with workload. The correlation is
\KeeperWorkloadR{} in the primary season at a p-value of \KeeperWorkloadP{}, and it
changes sign to \KeeperWorkloadRRepl{} in the replication season. A relationship whose
sign is not stable across two seasons is the strongest available form of a null result.

Goalkeeper clustering does not produce meaningful structure, and we report that rather
than presenting a partition. The same consensus rule selects \KeeperKPrimary{} clusters in
the primary season at a silhouette of \KeeperSilhouettePrimary{} but \KeeperKRepl{}
clusters in the replication season at \KeeperSilhouetteRepl{}, so the cluster count does
not replicate. The smallest replication cluster contains \KeeperMinClusterRepl{} members,
below the floor fixed before fitting. Most decisively, cluster membership explains more
variance in situational quantities than in goalkeeping ones: the proportion of variance
explained is \KeeperEtaTeamPoints{} for team points per match against \KeeperEtaSavePct{}
for save percentage. The partition recovers the strength of the team in front of the
goalkeeper, not the goalkeeper. No archetype names are assigned.

\subsection{Archetypes}
\label{sec:results:archetypes}

Because the selection rule returned two clusters in each position group, the study
describes six outfield archetypes rather than the richer taxonomy the design anticipated.
Their centroid profiles are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:archetypeprofiles} and their radar
comparisons in Figure~\ref{fig:radarpanels}. Names were assigned only after inspecting the
distinguishing features and the member players, and the justification for each is
reproduced from the generated record rather than composed for the paper.

Among defenders the split separates \NDFZero{} players with no positive attacking
signature at all from \NDFOne{} whose key passes, assists, expected assists and crosses
all sit around one standard deviation above the defensive mean. The first group is named
for what it does not do, because every one of its eight distinguishing features is
negative and its largest positive deviation is negligible. The second corresponds closely
to full backs and wing backs. Among midfielders the division is the cleanest in the study,
separating \NMFZero{} players whose only positive deviations are defensive or disciplinary
from \NMFOne{} whose shooting and creation measures are all elevated. Among forwards the
split distinguishes \NFWZero{} central players who shoot from \NFWOne{} wide players who
cross and create.

One structural property must be stated because it limits how much the six profiles can be
read as independent. Features are standardised within position group before clustering, so
with two clusters the two centroids in each group are necessarily anti-parallel: their
size-weighted sum is zero. Each position group therefore yields two ends of a single axis
rather than two independently shaped profiles, which is why the two members of each pair
mirror one another so exactly. The axis is real and the ordering along it is informative,
but the six archetypes represent three axes, not six independent types.

Outlier analysis is reported in Figure~\ref{fig:outliers}. The isolation forest and the
Mahalanobis distance agree closely, which is reassuring given that one is a partition
method and the other a distance method.

\subsection{What a supervised model sees}
\label{sec:results:supervised}

The clustering results leave one question open: are the surviving features too weak to
carry positional information at all, or do they carry it while the clustering chooses to
partition along a different axis? A supervised model settles it. Predicting the listed
position group from the same \NFeatures{} features, gradient boosting reaches an accuracy
of \LGBMAccuracy{} and a macro F1 of \LGBMMacroF{}, and logistic regression reaches
\LogitAccuracy{} and \LogitMacroF{}, against a majority class baseline of
\BaselineAccuracy{} and \BaselineMacroF{}. The features clearly do carry position, so the
weak agreement between clusters and positions reflects the clustering finding a different
and equally real axis, not an empty feature set. The confusion is almost entirely between
adjacent groups, as Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows, and forwards are the weakest class.

The SHAP decomposition in Figures~\ref{fig:shapbeeswarm} and \ref{fig:shapbyclass} carries
a finding that follows directly from the withdrawal. With possession and passing volume
gone, positions are identified largely by absence. Defenders are recognised by low
shooting and low key passes, forwards by low interceptions, and midfielders by an
intermediate profile on both. A classifier of this kind, trained before January 2026,
would have identified a defender by what he did with the ball. It now identifies him by
what he does not do.

Players the classifier places in the wrong group overlap significantly with the players
the clustering flagged as hybrids. The overlap rate is \OverlapLogit{} for logistic
regression at an enrichment of \EnrichmentLogit{} over independence, and
\OverlapLGBM{} at an enrichment of \EnrichmentLGBM{} for gradient boosting. The
enrichment is modest because the hybrid flag is itself broad, covering \NHybridPlayers{}
of \NEligiblePrimary{} players, so this is corroboration rather than a strong result.

The finishing above role regression illustrates a hazard worth recording. Predicting
non-penalty expected goals per ninety from non-shooting behaviour returns a cross
validated coefficient of determination of \FinishingRSq{}. An earlier specification that
also included xGChain returned \FinishingRSqLeaky{}, which looks far better and is in fact
circular: Understat credits xGChain for possessions the player finished himself, so the
difference between xGChain and xGBuildup contains the target and correlates with it at
\FinishingLeakCorr{}. The apparently strong model was reconstructing the outcome rather
than predicting it. Even at the honest value, roughly half the variance is unexplained, so
the residual leaderboard in Figure~\ref{fig:finishing} is presented as an illustration and
not as a measurement of finishing ability.

\subsection{Team signatures}
\label{sec:results:teams}

Aggregating players into clubs produces the one result in this study that is both strong
and stable. The minutes-weighted position of a club's outfield players on the first
principal component correlates with final league position at a Spearman coefficient of
\TeamPCOneRho{} in the primary season and \TeamPCOneRhoRepl{} in the replication season,
which is close to an exact reproduction. Since the first component is essentially
attacking involvement, this says that clubs whose players collectively shoot and create
more finish higher, which is unsurprising in itself, but the near identical coefficient
across two independent seasons shows the team level summary is measuring something stable
even where the player level partitions are not.

Hierarchical clustering on archetype composition groups the \NTeams{} clubs into
\NStyleGroups{} style families, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dendrogram}, with composition by
club in Figure~\ref{fig:composition}. Figure~\ref{fig:teampanels} places each club's
players in the shared embedding against the rest of the league, which makes squad shape
legible in a way that a composition bar cannot: clubs differ visibly in how far their
defenders sit toward the midfield region.

\subsection{Similarity, replication and noise}
\label{sec:results:novel}

\subsubsection{Player similarity}
Nearest neighbour search within position group recovers football sense for players whose
roles are defined by the surviving statistics, and fails informatively for players whose
roles are not. Full backs return full backs, penalty box forwards return penalty box
forwards, and centre backs return centre backs. The instructive failures are the
midfielders. A holding midfielder's nearest neighbours are creative attacking midfielders,
because with no tackles by pitch third, no blocks, no clearances, no pressures and no
passing volume, the only things left that distinguish him are his set piece deliveries and
key passes. The metric finds the players who share that signature and misses the entire
defensive half of his job. An inverted full back returns three centre backs for the same
reason, since the role is defined by possession behaviour that no longer exists in the
data. Similarity scores among centre backs are systematically the lowest in the table,
which is the same feature poverty appearing as an inability to tell defenders apart at
all. Figure~\ref{fig:similarity} shows the structure.

\subsubsection{Cross-season replication}
\NCommonPlayers{} players are eligible in both seasons. The declared rule independently
selects the same number of clusters in every scope in both seasons, so the structure
replicates. Membership replicates only partly. The adjusted Rand index between seasons is
\CrossSeasonARIGlobal{} globally, with \CrossSeasonChangedGlobal{} players changing
cluster, and within position group it falls to \CrossSeasonARIDF{} for defenders,
\CrossSeasonARIMF{} for midfielders and \CrossSeasonARIFW{} for forwards. The within group
figures are worse than the bootstrap stability of Section~\ref{sec:results:clustering},
which is what would be expected if the within group splits are finer than the data can
reliably support. Figure~\ref{fig:crossseason} shows the transitions.

\subsubsection{Sensitivity to sampling noise}
\label{sec:results:shrinkage}
Empirical Bayes shrinkage toward the position group mean, with the degree of shrinkage set
by minutes played, provides the sharpest test of whether the partitions describe playing
roles or sampling noise. Shrinkage is substantial rather than cosmetic, and the results
divide cleanly.

The global partition survives. Only \ShrinkChangedGlobal{} of \NEligiblePrimary{} players
change cluster, the adjusted Rand index between the original and shrunken partitions is
\ShrinkARIGlobal{}, the selected number of clusters is unchanged, and the rate at which
players move falls monotonically with minutes played, exactly as the noise account
predicts.

The within group partitions do not survive. The adjusted Rand index falls to
\ShrinkARIDF{} for defenders, with \ShrinkChangedDF{} of \NDFPrimary{} moving, and to
\ShrinkARIFW{} for forwards, while midfielders retain \ShrinkARIMF{}. The mechanism is
visible in the estimated variance components: several features are assigned an estimated
between-player variance of zero within a position group, meaning the model attributes
their entire observed spread to sampling noise and collapses them to the group mean,
removing them from the clustering altogether. Shot accuracy retains very little
reliability, because its true spread between players is small relative to the noise in
estimating it from a season of shots.

This matters because the scopes that dissolve under shrinkage are the same ones that had
weak bootstrap stability and weak cross-season replication. Three independent lines of
evidence point to the same conclusion, which is stated plainly here: the global division
between attacking and defensive involvement is a real property of players, while the finer
divisions within the defender and forward groups are substantially a description of
sampling noise and should not be used to characterise individual players.
Figures~\ref{fig:shrinkage} and \ref{fig:shrinkageminutes} show the effect.
