\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Is there any structure at all?}
\label{sec:results:structure}

The first question is not how many clusters there are but whether there are any.
Figure~\ref{fig:structurenull} answers it against the three clusterless references of
Section~\ref{sec:methods:null}.

Real structure exists, and it is confined to a single division. At two clusters the observed
silhouette is \StrObsAll{} for the outfield pool against a Gaussian null mean of
\StrNullAll{}, and it exceeds every one of the \StrSimulations{} simulations; the same holds
in each position group. A Gaussian is the softest reference available and those are the numbers
a sceptic should discount, so the two harder nulls are put through the same procedure. The copula
null keeps every feature's real marginal distribution, skew and bounds included,
along with the real rank correlations, which removes the objection that skew alone produced
the separation. The uniform reference keeps only the data's principal bounding box. The copula
is the hardest of the three in every scope, and the observed partition clears it everywhere,
by \StrZCopulaAll{} standard deviations on the pool and by \StrZCopulaDF{}, \StrZCopulaMF{}
and \StrZCopulaFW{} within the three position groups. The observed silhouette at two clusters
exceeds \StrBeatsEverywhere{} simulation under \StrBeatsEverywhere{} null in
\StrBeatsEverywhere{} scope, and the smallest margin anywhere over all four scopes and all
three nulls is \StrMinZOverall{} standard deviations, for forwards against the copula.

Beyond two clusters the curves converge, which is the central negative result of this paper
stated precisely. By around six clusters the observed silhouette lies inside the simulated
band in every position group, so the third, fourth and subsequent clusters that a taxonomy
would name are not distinguishable from what a partitioning algorithm extracts from
structureless data.

The second standard piece of evidence for a published taxonomy is a bootstrap, and the same
simulations show what it is worth. Computed on \StrBootDatasets{} clusterless datasets from
each null in each scope, by exactly the procedure applied to the real data, the bootstrap
adjusted Rand index of the two-cluster partition runs from \StrBootNullMin{} to
\StrBootNullMax{}, and no single clusterless dataset anywhere falls below
\StrBootNullMinSingle{}. The observed data run from \StrBootObsMin{} to \StrBootObsMax{}. The
gap between real data and data built to contain no groups is as small as \StrBootGapMin{} and
never larger than \StrBootGapMax{}, and a stability screen at \StrBootScreen{}, demanding by
the genre's standards, is passed by \StrBootNullPassesScreen{} clusterless dataset in
\StrBootNullPassesScreen{} scope. A resampling check does not distinguish real structure from
a partitioned continuum. It measures whether an algorithm is deterministic on similar inputs,
which $k$-means is, and it is reported here only because readers will expect it.

Three further instruments agree, with one exception reported as such. Hartigan's dip test
rejects unimodality on the leading principal component at $p\,\StrDipPAll{}$ for the pool, but
at these sample sizes a p-value rejects for departures too small to matter, so the dip
statistic is the quantity to read and it is read against the nulls. The observed dip on the
pool is \StrDipObsAll{}, which sits \StrDipMinZAll{} null standard deviations above the
hardest of the three references; defenders clear it by \StrDipMinZDF{} and midfielders by
\StrDipMinZMF{}, so the dominant axis is two-moded rather than merely wide. Forwards are the
exception in both readings: the test returns $p=\StrDipPFW{}$, and the observed dip of
\StrDipObsFW{} sits \StrDipMinZFW{} standard deviations against the uniform reference, which
is to say below it. The forward split is real by the silhouette calibration, clearing the
hardest null by \StrMinZFW{}, and its leading axis is not bimodal by the dip. Both halves are
reported because together they say what the forward split is: a cut through a continuum of
involvement that the null cannot produce, rather than two kinds of forward. Density-based
clustering assigns \StrNoiseAll{} of the pool to noise and every position group entirely to
noise, which is what a density method does when handed a continuum with a density gradient
instead of separated groups. The Gaussian mixture criterion declines monotonically to the top
of the candidate range in every scope, the signature of a model absorbing extra components
into a smooth density rather than identifying real ones.

Taken together the instruments support one claim and refuse a stronger one. Football role data
contains a real division into two modes. It does not contain a taxonomy.

\subsection{The shape of the space, and what the modes are not}
\label{sec:results:geometry}

A two-mode description invites two objections. If the data reduce to two groups, perhaps they
reduce to one number. If they do not, perhaps the two groups are the listed positions under
another name. Figure~\ref{fig:geometry} answers both, and the answer to each is no.

The leading principal component carries \ArcPCOne{} percent of the variance, far more than any
other single direction, which confirms that one axis dominates. It is nowhere near the whole
space. The first three components together reach only \ArcPCThree{} percent, and
\ArcPCNinety{} are required to reach ninety percent of the variance, so the role space has one
dominant axis and a long tail of smaller ones, which is a different object from a line. The
content of the leading components matches the football. The first separates players by where on the pitch they
touch the ball, loading positively on progressive receptions and touches in the attacking
third and the opposition box, and negatively on touches in the defensive third and in the
defending team's own box. The second is a progression axis close to orthogonal to the first. A deep
midfielder who moves the ball forward and a striker who receives it in the box sit at opposite
ends of the second component while sharing territory on the first. That pair explains how two modes and a
multidimensional space coexist: the first component is bimodal and drives the
partition, while the components behind it carry real and interpretable variation that is
continuous rather than clustered.

Projecting every player onto the axis between the two centroids shows the distribution the
paper is describing. Density is concentrated at two ends with a populated middle between them,
and the listed positions are laid across that shape rather than aligned with it. Agreement
with the listed position group is well above chance and well below recovery, at an adjusted
Rand index of \ArcARIvsPosition{} and a normalised mutual information of \ArcNMIvsPosition{}.
A partition that simply relabelled position would return values near
one and a partition unrelated to position would return values near zero.

Where the line falls is the informative part. \ArcSplitDF{} percent of defenders fall on one
side and \ArcSplitFW{} percent of forwards on the other, while midfielders divide
\ArcModeAttMF{} to \ArcModeDefMF{}, which puts \ArcSplitMF{} percent of them on the attacking
side and is as close to an even split as the sample permits. It is that even split that drags
the agreement indices down. The partition is not a relabelling of position but a cut through
it: defenders and forwards sit at the ends of the same axis and are assigned almost
deterministically, while midfielders are spread along its middle and assigned by which end
they lean toward.

A supervised model recovers the same geometry from machinery that shares nothing with the
clustering. Gradient boosting predicts the listed position group at \SupGBMAcc{} percent
accuracy and a macro F1 of \SupGBMF{} out of fold, against a majority-class baseline of
\SupBaseline{} percent, and multinomial logistic regression is barely behind at
\SupLogitAcc{} percent. The near equality of a linear model and a boosted tree ensemble is
itself informative, since it means almost all the recoverable signal is linearly separable and
there is little interaction structure left for the trees to find, where a taxonomy of many
discrete types would be expected to leave more. The errors are where the argument is. Of
\SupN{} player-seasons the model assigns \SupDFasFW{} defenders to the forward class and
\SupFWasDF{} forwards to the defender class, while every other confusion runs through midfield,
which is involved in \SupMiddleErrors{} errors against the \SupPoleErrors{} that link the two
poles directly. Only \SupPoleShare{} percent of misclassifications skip the middle. That is the
confusion structure of an ordered line whose ends are far apart and whose middle overlaps both,
not of three distinct categories, which would scatter errors more evenly across the
off-diagonal. The feature attributions agree: the three strongest features overall are
\ShapArcTopOne{}, \ShapArcTopTwo{} and \ShapArcTopThree{}, all territorial rather than
technical. Position, as the data record it, is mostly a statement about where a player stands.

\subsection{What the two modes are}
\label{sec:results:archetypes}

Two modes within each of three position groups give six descriptions, and with \ArcFeatures{}
features they can be characterised sharply. Names were assigned only after inspecting centroid
profiles and member players, and the justifications are generated from the computed centroids
rather than composed for the paper. Figure~\ref{fig:modes} shows the features that separate
each pair.

Among defenders the division is between \ArcNDFZero{} \ArcNameDFZero{} and \ArcNDFOne{}
\ArcNameDFOne{}. The first take three times as many touches in their own defensive third as
the second, more than twice as many in their own box, twice the possession-adjusted clearances,
and win considerably more aerial duels. The second take five times as many touches in the
attacking third, attempt ten times as many crosses, receive far more progressive passes and
attempt four times as many take-ons. This is the centre back against wide defender
distinction, and the notable point is that the source's own position label does not encode it,
since the great majority of both groups are listed simply as defenders.

Among midfielders the division separates \ArcNMFZero{} \ArcNameMFZero{} from \ArcNMFOne{}
\ArcNameMFOne{}. The first recover the ball more often, tackle more and take far more touches
in their own third. The instructive detail is that they also make more passes into the final
third and more progressive passes than the second group. They are not less involved in
progressing the ball, they progress it differently, by passing forward from deep rather than by
receiving high. A description that treated attacking involvement as one axis would miss that,
and it is the clearest case in the paper of the richer feature set buying something a poorer
one could not.

Among forwards the division separates \ArcNFWZero{} \ArcNameFWZero{} from \ArcNFWOne{}
\ArcNameFWOne{}, and it is not a division by scoring. Non-penalty expected goals are slightly
higher in the first group. What separates them is carrying and creation, with the second group
recording nearly three times the progressive carries, almost three times the passes into the
box, twice the take-ons and substantially more shot creating actions while shooting slightly
less. Forward is not one role, but the axis that splits it is involvement in build-up rather
than goal threat.

One structural property constrains how far the six can be read as independent. Features are
standardised within position group before a two-way split, so the two centroids in a group are
exactly anti-parallel and the larger cluster necessarily carries the smaller deviations. Each
pair is therefore the two ends of one axis, and the six archetypes describe three axes rather
than six independently shaped profiles. The three are also not equally well supported. The
defender split is the strongest on every measure applied to it: the largest centroid
separations, the lowest churn, the highest agreement across leagues, and a dip test that
rejects unimodality. The midfield split is well supported with a more populated boundary. The
forward split is the weakest and should be read with the most caution, since its dip test does
not reject unimodality, the division is uneven, and one league's refit falls well below the
others.

\subsection{The same division in every season, league and feature family}
\label{sec:results:replication}

A partition that appears once is a description of one dataset. A partition that appears
independently in five seasons and five competitions is a description of the sport. Each season
and each league is therefore standardised inside itself and clustered from scratch, borrowing
no centre, no scale and no starting point from the pooled fit.

Every scope recovers the pooled partition. The weakest season agrees with the pool at an
adjusted Rand index of \RepSeasonPooledMin{} and the weakest league at \RepLeaguePooledMin{},
on \RepN{} player-seasons in total. The separating axis is the same object each time rather
than merely a similarly sized one, since the correlation between each scope's own discriminant
direction and the pooled direction never falls below \RepAxisCorrMin{}. Importing one scope's
centroids into another scope's players separates replication of the solution from change in the
players, and it is the cleaner of the two tests: across the twenty ordered league pairs the
transferred centroids reproduce the receiving league's own fit at a mean adjusted Rand index of
\RepTransferMean{}, never falling below \RepTransferMin{}. A model of role fitted on Ligue 1
assigns Bundesliga players almost exactly as a model fitted on Bundesliga does.

The season-against-season comparison on players present in both is lower, at a mean of
\RepPairMean{} with a range from \RepPairMin{} to \RepPairMax{}, and the pattern explains why.
Agreement decays monotonically with the gap between seasons while the transfer index over the
same pairs stays flat, so the decay is players changing role over four years rather than a
weakness of the partition. Across the same pairs \RepShareMean{} percent of players present in
both seasons are assigned to the same mode in both, falling no lower than \RepShareMin{}
percent on the pair furthest apart in time. Roughly one player in twelve moves between modes
over a four-year span, which is a plausible rate of genuine role change and not the churn an
unstable partition would produce. Figure~\ref{fig:replication} shows all of this, the
season and league refits above and the feature-family ablation below.

A result on \ArcFeatures{} features could also be carried by one block of them, and a reviewer
who suspected the modes were the expected goals block wearing a costume would be right to ask.
The features fall into \AblFamilies{} families, and each was removed in turn and each was tried
alone. Removing any one family removes nothing that matters. The rule returns two clusters in
\AblSurvivesAll{} leave-one-out in every scope, and of the \AblCells{} feature sets tested
across four scopes, \AblCellsBeatNull{} exceed every one of the \AblSimulations{} clusterless
simulations; the weakest case is \AblWeakScope{} without \AblWeakFamily{}, where the observed
silhouette still sits \AblWeakZ{} null standard deviations above the clusterless mean. The
survivors are the same two modes rather than two different ones, since the adjusted Rand index
between each ablated partition and the full one runs from \AblLeaveARIMin{} to
\AblLeaveARIMax{}.

The converse is more instructive than the headline. Judged by whether a single family produces
a two-cluster solution that beats the null, most families carry structure alone, and
\AblCarryEvery{} do so in every scope. That criterion is generous, and the agreement with the
full partition shows why. Shooting alone in defenders separates from the null at
\AblKeepZShootingDF{} standard deviations while agreeing with the paper's partition at only
\AblKeepARIShootingDF{}: it finds two groups, and they are the finishers against everyone else,
not the deep against the wide. Only territory reproduces the paper's own modes by itself in
every scope, at an adjusted Rand index from \AblTerritoryARIMin{} to \AblTerritoryARIMax{}. The
two modes are where a player touches the ball, and every other family either sharpens that
division or, alone, cuts a different one. The two failures are forwards, where shooting alone
clears the null by only \AblKeepZShootingFW{} standard deviations and passing accuracy alone
reaches \AblKeepZPassingFW{} without exceeding every simulation, which is a further reason to
read the forward split as a cut through a continuum of involvement.

\subsection{Do the modes predict results?}
\label{sec:results:outcomes}

Everything above is a claim about the structure of the data. The archive also carries what
happened, so the two modes can be tested against real results and not only against listed
positions. Each of \OutN{} team-seasons is summarised by the minutes-weighted share of its
outfield minutes played by the attacking mode, by the minutes-weighted mean position of its
players along the axis between the two centroids, and by its share in each of the six
archetypes, with eligible players covering a median \OutCoverageMedian{} percent of each club's
outfield minutes and never less than \OutCoverageMin{}.

The share alone correlates with points per match at a Spearman rank correlation of
\OutRhoShare{} (\OutRhoShareLow{} to \OutRhoShareHigh{}) and with goal difference per match at
\OutRhoShareGD{}. The continuous axis position is stronger, at \OutRhoAxis{}
(\OutRhoAxisLow{} to \OutRhoAxisHigh{}) against points, which is what a continuum should show:
where a club's players sit along the axis carries more than which side of the cut they fall on.
Within leagues the share correlation runs from \OutRhoLeagueMin{} in \OutRhoLeagueMinName{} to
\OutRhoLeagueMax{} in \OutRhoLeagueMaxName{}, and every season lies inside that range.
Table~\ref{tab:outcomes} lists the ten team-seasons at the extremes so a reader can check them
against memory, and they are the sides one would name.

A correlation between attacking composition and points is partly circular, because good teams
have good attackers, so the test that matters is whether the modes carry anything beyond team
possession, the simplest summary of dominance. Ridge regression of points per match on
possession alone, on the composition alone and on both gives out-of-sample coefficients of
determination of \OutRSqPossSeason{}, \OutRSqCompSeason{} and \OutRSqBothSeason{} when one
season is held out at a time, and \OutRSqPossLeague{}, \OutRSqCompLeague{} and
\OutRSqBothLeague{} when one league is. The strictest holdout leaves one club out at a time, so
that no held-out team-season shares a club with the training data and a model cannot score by
remembering which clubs are good; there the three figures are \OutRSqPossClub{},
\OutRSqCompClub{} and \OutRSqBothClub{}. The modes add \OutIncSeason{} (\OutIncSeasonLow{} to
\OutIncSeasonHigh{}) to what possession explains under the first holdout, \OutIncLeague{} under
the second and \OutIncClub{} under the club holdout. The composition on its own predicts
slightly worse than possession on its own, which should be said plainly: the modes are not a
substitute for dominance, they are information about how it is produced that dominance does not
contain.

Where the information lives is the sharpest result in this section. Possession plus the two
two-mode measures alone reach \OutRSqPossTwoModeSeason{}, an increment of
\OutIncTwoModeSeason{} over possession that is the whole of the gain. Adding the six archetype
shares on top adds \OutIncArcheSeason{} (\OutIncArcheSeasonLow{} to \OutIncArcheSeasonHigh{}),
an interval that contains zero, and \OutIncArcheClub{} under the club holdout, while the
archetypes without the two-mode measures reach only \OutRSqPossArcheSeason{}. Real results say
what the null calibration said: two modes carry information and a finer taxonomy carries none
that the two modes did not already carry. Figure~\ref{fig:outcomes} shows the share against
points and the out of sample comparison.

\subsection{Goalkeepers}
\label{sec:results:keepers}

Goalkeepers are described by a different vocabulary from outfield players, and the primary
sample supplies all of it, with \ArcKeeperSeasons{} keeper-seasons carrying post-shot expected
goals, sweeper actions and their average distance, cross stopping, launch rate and average pass
length, at a worst per-column coverage of \ArcKeeperCoverageMin{}.

The first attempt at a partition walks into a trap, and the trap is itself a finding. Clustering
on all available features returns \ArcKeeperK{} clusters at a silhouette of
\ArcKeeperSilFull{}, and the partition looks interpretable until it is asked what it is made
of. Membership explains a mean of \ArcKeeperEtaFullSituation{} of the variance in situational
quantities, the volume and quality of shooting a defence permits, against
\ArcKeeperEtaFullTechnique{} in quantities describing what the goalkeeper does. The partition
is largely a ranking of the teams in front of the goalkeepers. Named as archetypes it would be
a league table wearing a tactical vocabulary. Excluding the exposure variables and clustering
on technique alone reverses that completely. The same rule again returns \ArcKeeperK{}
clusters, at a silhouette of \ArcKeeperSilTech{} and a bootstrap adjusted Rand index of
\ArcKeeperBootARI{}, and membership now explains \ArcKeeperEtaTechTechnique{} of the variance
in technique against \ArcKeeperEtaTechSituation{} in situation, a ratio of about seven.

What that partition describes is not the expected taxonomy. The familiar trio of shot stopper,
sweeper keeper and distributor is not what the data produces. The division is between
\ArcKeeperNameZero{}s, \ArcKeeperNZero{} keeper-seasons who stay on their line, and
\ArcKeeperNameOne{}s, \ArcKeeperNOne{} who come off it, separating on cross stopping and
sweeping together. Distribution style barely separates the clusters at all, and neither does
shot stopping ability. Both are real and measurable, and both are continua lying across the
off-line division rather than dimensions of it. A goalkeeper who sweeps is not thereby a better
or worse shot stopper, and how far he kicks the ball is close to independent of both.

The results test discriminates between the two partitions in the direction the effect sizes
predict. The share of a club's goalkeeper minutes in the full-feature partition's second
cluster correlates with points per match at \OutKeepFull{} (\OutKeepFullLow{} to
\OutKeepFullHigh{}), which is what a partition sorting keepers by the team in front of them
must do. The technique partition's share correlates at \OutKeepTech{} (\OutKeepTechLow{} to
\OutKeepTechHigh{}), an interval that barely clears zero, so the line keeper against sweeper
claimer division is unrelated to how good the team is, which is the property a description of
goalkeeping rather than of goalkeepers' teams should have. Figure~\ref{fig:keepers} shows the technique partition and
the two profiles. That is a substantive finding rather than a taxonomy, and it echoes the
outfield result: the data support one binary distinction plus continuous variation, not a set
of types.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:results:robustness}

Four checks bound what the result depends on, and each is reported in full in the supplementary
material.

The result does not depend on the richness of the description. The reduced sample of
Section~\ref{sec:methods:data} provides an unusually clean test, because the withdrawal removed
a specific and large part of the feature set without touching the population. Repeating the
analysis there returns the same answer: the declared rule selects two clusters globally and
within every position group, agreement with listed positions remains weak at
\ARIvsPosition{}, and density-based clustering again assigns every position group entirely to
noise. Removing expected goals, all passing and possession volume, shot and goal creating
actions and the entire advanced goalkeeping table changes what the two modes are made of
without changing that there are two of them, which bounds what richer data buys: the modern
feature block sharpens the description considerably but reveals no additional modes that the
poorer description was hiding.

The result does not depend on the eligibility threshold either. Repeating the entire selection
and stability procedure at 270, \MinMinutes{} and 900 minutes, treating each threshold as an
independent pool, returns two clusters at every threshold with the silhouette moving only from
\SensSilhouetteLow{} to \SensSilhouetteHigh{}, and the partition itself agreeing with the one
reported here at \SensAgreeLow{} and \SensAgreeHigh{} on the common players
(Section~\ref{sec:si:sensitivity}).

The possession adjustment behind the defensive features is measured rather than assumed, and
the measurement matters. Four of the five defensive elasticities have bootstrap intervals
excluding one, so the conventional proportional correction overcorrects them; for clearances
the interval reaches one and the paper does not claim otherwise. Applying the conventional
exponent reverses the sign of the residual correlation with team possession rather than
removing it. The correction is also one sided, and that asymmetry is tested rather than
asserted: attacking output responds to a team's own possession more than proportionately, so
dividing it out would remove signal along with any bias. Section~\ref{sec:si:elasticity} gives
the estimates, the intervals, the league refits and the counterfactual in which both sides are
corrected.

Finally, the partitions are not artefacts of sampling noise in per-90 rates computed over
partial seasons. Empirical Bayes shrinkage toward the position group mean, with the degree of
shrinkage set by minutes played, leaves the global partition essentially unchanged, while the
within-group splits survive unequally; Section~\ref{sec:si:reduced} reports which, and reports
the within-season role drift analysis that bounds the assumption that a player's role is
constant across a season.
